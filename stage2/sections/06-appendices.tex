\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}

$v$             & class values, $v=(-1,-\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12,1)$ \\
$s_{d,k}$       & signed stance of $d$ on topic $k$, $s_{d,k}\in[-1,1]$ \\
\end{tabular}

\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}

\end{tabular}

For these tensors, high sparsity is expected because absence of evidence is recorded as missing, not as zero.

\section*{Appendix E: Extended literature and method notes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix E: Extended literature and method notes}
\label{app:e}

\paragraph{The gap, literature by literature.}
The descriptive work of Section~\ref{sec:computational} \parencite{Gardiner2025,Mancilla2023}
shows what the regime outputs but not who produces what. The structural analyses of co-authorship
networks \parencite{Botelho_CoalitionDynamics,Kumar_ConsistentPatterns} are undirected and
unattributed. The text-based positional methods of Section~\ref{sec:computational} recover latent
positions rather than topic-specific stances over time (\hyperref[app:g]{Appendix~G}). Finally,
consensus has been simulated as convergence (Section~\ref{sec:consensus}) or on synthetic data
(Section~\ref{sec:marl}).

\paragraph{ATCM document and output types.}
The ATCM's inputs and outputs, including the paper types beyond Working Papers, are described in
\hyperref[app:f]{Appendix~F}.

\paragraph{Responsive (opinion-dynamics) model variants.}
Examples are the Kuramoto and opinion-changing-rate models used by
\textcite{Botelho_CoalitionDynamics}, synchronization models that represent agreement as a
coupled-oscillator convergence, and the bounded-confidence \parencite{Hegselmann2002} and
stubborn-agent \parencite{FriedkinJohnsen1990} variants of the iterative-averaging scheme of
\textcite{DeGroot1974}.

\paragraph{Veto-players condition \parencite{Tsebelis2002}.}
Let $q$ denote the status quo, $p$ a proposal, and $u_i$ the utility function of party $i$,
which scores how much that party values each policy. The status quo is replaced only when
$u_i(p) \geq u_i(q)$ for every party $i$: no party may be left worse off than it is now.

\paragraph{Foundational MARL literature.}
Foundational MARL work covers multi-issue bargaining \parencite{Chang2021}, negotiating-team
formation \parencite{Bachrach2020}, bargaining under risk and asymmetric information
\parencite{Schmid2020}, and search-improved game-theoretic learning \parencite{Li2023}. In these
systems, neural networks (NN) enter only as function approximators, allowing the learning to
scale to large state and action spaces.

\paragraph{LLM-based negotiation simulations.}
\textcite{Moghimifar2024} simulate coalition formation in parliament using text data from party
manifestos. \textcite{Bolleddu2025} present a MARL system for consensus building and negotiation
based on synthetic data. \textcite{Kulkarni2025} use negotiated agreements with game-theoretic
reasoning to predict dynamic coalition formation in Diplomacy, a board game in which seven powers
negotiate, form, and break alliances, widely used as a benchmark for this type of simulation.
Some benchmarks for real negotiation data are starting to appear \parencite{Benac2026}.

\paragraph{Wordfish \parencite{Slapin2008}.}
Its aim was to estimate party positions over time without hand-coded reference texts. The model
treats each word count as a Poisson draw whose expected frequency depends on the party's position
on a single latent dimension, and it recovered credible left--right trajectories for German
parties from their election manifestos (1990--2005).

\paragraph{Wordshoal \parencite{Lauderdale2016}.}
Their model scales each debate separately with Wordfish and then combines the debate-level
positions into a general dimension through a Bayesian factor analysis. Applied to speeches in the
Irish D\'ail and the US Senate, it recovered within-party disagreement that voting records hide.
Each debate scale is whatever axis dominates that debate's word frequencies, which need not be
the substantive for-or-against axis of interest. Lauderdale and Herzog get away with this because
legislative debates are adversarial, so the dominant axis is usually government versus
opposition; ATCM Working Papers have no such structure.

\paragraph{BERTopic and NLI models.}
BERTopic \parencite{Grootendorst_BERTopic} clusters document embeddings and labels each cluster
with its most distinctive words. Natural language inference (NLI) models
\parencite{Burnham2023,Abercrombie2022} are classifiers that take a text and a stated hypothesis
(e.g. ``this paper supports stricter protection'') and judge whether the text entails,
contradicts, or is neutral towards it. A single stable ideal point is too coarse for the ATCM,
where a party can support stricter environmental protection yet oppose new tourism rules.

\paragraph{Support-layer pipeline details.}
Each document is embedded: mapped to a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{m}$, under the geometric assumption
that documents with similar meaning receive nearby vectors. BERTopic reduces the dimension of
these vectors and clusters them, and each cluster is a topic; each centroid
$\boldsymbol{\mu}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ is the average embedding of the documents in topic $k$.
Each document $d$ is split into passages $P_d = \{p_1, \dots, p_{n_d}\}$, and each passage $p$ is
embedded as $\mathbf{e}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$. Relevance is defined quantitatively:
$\rho_{p,k} = \cos(\mathbf{e}_p, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)$, the cosine similarity between passage
embedding and topic centroid, so a passage that points in the same direction as the topic
centroid discusses that topic. Relevance acts as a gate: $P_{d,k}$ is the set of passages scoring
above a threshold, and only these pass to the next stage. The topic-relevant sentences in each
$P_{d,k}$ are extracted using a Large Language Model (LLM); $\mathrm{Extract}(P_{d,k})$ denotes
this step. The extracted sentences are concatenated and scored as a set by the NLI classifier:
given the text as premise and a hypothesis about topic $k$, it scores entailment, neutrality, and
contradiction. Entailment means the premise gives support to the hypothesis. Contradiction means
the premise contradicts it. The resulting scores are binned and combined into five ordinal
classes,
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{d,k} = \mathrm{NLI}\big(\mathrm{Extract}(P_{d,k})\big)
            \in \Delta_5, \qquad
  \Delta_5 = \Big\{\, \pi \in \mathbb{R}^{5} \;\big|\;
            \pi_\ell \ge 0,\ \textstyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{5}\pi_\ell = 1 \,\Big\},
\label{eq:stance-dist}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_5$ is the probability simplex: $\pi_{d,k}$ assigns a non-negative probability to
each of the five classes, and these probabilities sum to one. The signed stance is the expected
value of this distribution under an equal-spacing convention,
\begin{equation}
  s_{d,k} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{5} v_\ell\,\pi_{d,k}(\ell),
  \qquad v = \big(-1,\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,0,\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,1\big).
  \label{eq:stance}
\end{equation}
Since $\pi_{d,k}$ is a probability distribution and the five fixed class values $v_\ell$ all lie
in $[-1,1]$, the stance $s_{d,k}$ is a convex combination of those values. The stance is
therefore a continuous quantity in $[-1,1]$ on topic $k$'s axis, while $v$ itself never varies.

\paragraph{Recovering stance as a contrast (Chapter~1).}
Measuring directional stance in diplomatic text is challenging because diplomats hedge: cautious,
non-committal phrasing and a shared vocabulary of safeguards pull single-hypothesis scores toward
neutrality. A party rarely writes ``we oppose this measure'', it writes ``further consideration
may be warranted'', and a phrase such as ``subject to appropriate environmental safeguards''
appears in permissive and restrictive papers alike. Stance is therefore recovered as a contrast.
For each issue, three hypotheses are written: a supporting pole, a neutral one, and an opposing
pole (e.g. $h_k^{+}$ ``tourism should be further regulated'', $h_k^{-}$ ``tourism regulation
should be relaxed''). Each is paired with the extracted
text as premise and scored by the NLI classifier \parencite{Laurer2024}, and the against-pole
score is subtracted from the for-pole score, giving a direction that survives the non-committal
phrasing. As a robustness check, the same texts are scored a second time by a generative LLM used
as a secondary instrument \parencite{LeMens2024}.

\paragraph{Stance classifier (Chapter~1).}
For topic $k$, let $h_k^{+}, h_k^{0}, h_k^{-}$ be the for-, neutral-, and
against-pole hypotheses. For passage set $d$ and hypothesis $h$, the debiased evidence is the null-premise-corrected log-ratio,
\[
  \lambda(d,h) = \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(
     \log P(\text{entail}\mid d,h) - \log P(\text{entail}\mid \varnothing,h),
     \ \pm 5 \Big),
\]
this removes each hypothesis's marginal acceptability. The three-pole distribution
combines entailment of each pole with contradiction of its opposite,
\[
  \tilde\pi_{d,k} = \operatorname{softmax}\!\begin{pmatrix}
     \lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}(h_k^{+}) + \lambda_{\mathrm{con}}(h_k^{-}) \\[2pt]
     \lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}(h_k^{0}) \\[2pt]
     \lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}(h_k^{-}) + \lambda_{\mathrm{con}}(h_k^{+})
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
these values are reduced to a continuous score in $[-1,1]$: the against-pole probability
subtracted from the for-pole probability. The score is binned into the five ordinal classes of
$\pi_{d,k}$ at the midpoints of the class values $v$: scores in $[-1,-0.75)$ are strongly
against, $[-0.75,-0.25)$ against, $[-0.25,0.25)$ neutral, $[0.25,0.75)$ in favour, and
$[0.75,1]$ strongly in favour. The scalar stance is then the expected value of
Equation~\ref{eq:stance}.

\paragraph{Aggregation (Chapter~1).}
The document stances are aggregated over the Working Papers, weighted by co-authorship credit,
\begin{equation}
  S_{c,k,y} = \sum_{\substack{d \in D(c,y) \\ k \in K(d)}}
              w_{d,c}\, s_{d,k},
  \label{eq:aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $S_{c,k,y}$ is the support of country $c$ on topic $k$ in year $y$, $D(c,y)$ is the set of
Working Papers co-authored by country $c$ in year $y$, $K(d)$ is the set of topics assigned to
document $d$, and $w_{d,c}$ is the attribution weight of that country in one document. For a
fixed year $y$, the slice $S_{\cdot,\cdot,y} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times |K|}$ is a signed
$\text{country} \times \text{topic}$ matrix, with $C$ countries and $|K|$ topics; a dot means
the index runs over all its values.

\paragraph{Attribution weights (Chapter~1).}
Two attribution conventions are computed:
\begin{itemize}
      \item Full credit, $w_{d,c} = 1$: every co-author fully holds the position. This is the
      coalition-membership reading (``who is on this side?'').
      \item Fractional credit, $w_{d,c} = 1/|A_d|$, with $A_d$ the co-authors of $d$: the
      paper's positional mass is divided among its authors, discounting states that merely sign
      on. This is the influence reading (``who is driving this?'').
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Other ideal-point precedents.}
\textcite{Vafa2020} produced a probabilistic framework that jointly places ideal points and
models actors' language.

\paragraph{Embeddings.}
An embedding may cover a word, a sentence, or a whole document. The map is learned from large
corpora so that texts appearing in similar contexts receive nearby vectors. Neural language
models such as BERT \parencite{Devlin2019} make the vectors context-dependent: the same word
receives different vectors in different sentences.

\paragraph{The UN General Debate corpus \parencite{Baturo2017}.}
Their corpus collects over 7300 annual General Debate statements (1970--2014), one per country
per year, in a setting that parallels the ATCM: formal, annual, state-level, and translated into
official languages. They derive preferences in two ways, both from word frequencies: Wordscores, a
supervised scaling in which the analyst defines the axis in advance through reference texts (US
and Russian statements anchor a US--Russia dimension), and correspondence analysis, an
unsupervised reduction whose dimensions are interpreted afterwards.

\paragraph{Opposition tensor (Chapter~2).}
$O_{c,k,y} \in [0,1]$ is the opposition of country $c$ on topic $k$ in year $y$, built from the
extracted \emph{(country, target)} pairs: $0$ means the country is not likely at all
to veto, and $1$ means a certain veto.

\paragraph{Simulation-layer equations (Chapter~3).}
Each agent's action on a presented proposal is binary, $o_i \in \{0,1\}$: one if agent $i$
objects, zero otherwise. Under the consensus rule the proposal is adopted only if no Party
objects,
\begin{equation}
  1_{\mathrm{adopt}} = \prod_{j} \big(1 - o_j\big),
  \label{eq:adopt}
\end{equation}
therefore, a single objection vetoes adoption. The reward to agent $i$ is then
\begin{equation}
  R_i = \alpha\, S_{i,k,y}\, 1_{\mathrm{adopt}}
      - \beta\, O_{i,k,y}\, 1_{\mathrm{adopt}}
      - \gamma\, o_i,
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where the first term rewards adoption of outcomes the Party supports, the second penalises
adoption of outcomes it opposes, the third subtracts a diplomatic cost for casting an objection,
and $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \ge 0$ are trade-off weights, learned during training to fit the historical consensus record.

\section*{Appendix F: The ATs as a consensus regime}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix F: The ATs as a consensus regime}
\label{app:f}

At the ATCM, Consultative Parties (CPs) reach decisions exclusively by consensus; non-consultative
parties (NCPs), observers, and experts attend but take no part. These outputs take three forms
\parencite[Rule~24]{ATS2023RoP}. \textbf{Measures} set out substantive provisions that the
parties intend to be legally binding in their domestic law; they require each party's government
to formally approve them. \textbf{Decisions} settle the ATCM's own internal and organisational
matters. \textbf{Resolutions} are non-binding: they record recommendations or expressions of
intent, with no legal force.

There are four types of input. The one that matters for this thesis is the \textbf{Working Paper
(WP)} \parencite[Rule~48]{ATS2023RoP}: submitted by CPs and Observers, WPs require discussion at
the ATCM and are the main source of outputs. They are the only input from CPs that can produce an
outcome. \textbf{Secretariat Papers} \parencite[Rule~49]{ATS2023RoP} cover organisational matters
such as the budget. \textbf{Information Papers} \parencite[Rule~50]{ATS2023RoP} provide
supporting information, also from NCPs and experts. \textbf{Background Papers}
\parencite[Rule~51]{ATS2023RoP} are documents submitted for the record only.

The consensus system makes the ATs a notable example of international cooperation
\parencite{jackson2021antarctica}; however, the same mechanism has become a major challenge as
the number of CPs grows \parencite{Tsebelis2002,Gardiner2025}.

A notable case of contested consensus occurred in the late 1970s and 1980s, during the
negotiations over mineral resources in Antarctica. The Convention on the Regulation of Antarctic
Mineral Resource Activities (CRAMRA) was a framework, agreed in 1988, that would have permitted
and regulated mining in Antarctica. It was abandoned before entering into force and replaced by
the Madrid Protocol (1991), which instead prohibited all mineral resource extraction and
designated Antarctica a natural reserve devoted to peace and science
\parencite{jackson2021antarctica}. Although it unfolded in two stages, it forms a single
documented episode: one negotiation over Antarctic mineral resources in which the parties'
positions are recorded and then reversed. That documented reversal makes it a valuable canary
test, a known case against which any quantitative stance measure can be validated.

\section*{Appendix G: Measuring policy preference from political text}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix G: Measuring policy preference from political text}
\label{app:g}

Positional measures place each actor on a policy scale, for example from favouring regulation
to favouring access, using only the text the actor produces. Extracting them from political
texts is a well-developed field with two paradigms. They differ in how they turn text into
numbers: the first paradigm uses word counts (i.e. frequencies), while the second uses embeddings.
The first paradigm is scaling and ideal-point methods, which recover a latent positional dimension
from the data itself: the axis is not chosen in advance, it emerges from patterns in the text,
and its meaning is interpreted after estimation. Wordfish \parencite{Slapin2008} is a Poisson
scaling model of party text (\hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). Its limitation is that the recovered dimension is
uninterpreted: it is the dominant axis of variation in word usage, and in a corpus as topically
diverse as the ATCM Working Papers it would likely reflect what parties talk about rather than
what they support. \textcite{Lauderdale2016} addressed the problem that
legislators talk about different things in different debates by scaling each debate separately,
but the debate scales inherit the same uninterpreted-dimension problem, and the debates must be
given in advance (\hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). Two things are therefore missing for this thesis: the topics have
to be discovered from the papers themselves, and the output must be a stance per topic rather
than one general dimension.
The closest dataset to the ATCM corpus is the UN debate corpus \textcite{Baturo2017} use to
derive state-level preferences, in a setting that parallels the ATCM (\hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). Finally,
\textcite{Parschan2025} review the text-based ideal-point estimation algorithms of this first
paradigm, comparing their assumptions and variants in one place.
The second paradigm uses embeddings as its main tool. An embedding maps a piece of text to a
vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$ so that texts with similar meaning receive nearby vectors: comparisons
of meaning become geometric operations. Modern embeddings are produced by neural language models
such as BERT \parencite{Devlin2019} (details in \hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). Word-embedding geometry recovers
ideological placement in parliamentary data \parencite{Rheault2019}, but the choice of embeddings
strongly affects applied inference and the results must be validated \parencite{Rodriguez2021}.

This thesis adopts the second paradigm and uses embeddings at several stages: to discover what
topics the Working Papers discuss, to measure how relevant each paper is to each topic, and,
through the same family of neural models, to read each paper's stance on a topic. This yields
the topic-conditioned stance that the first paradigm cannot supply: a separate position per
party and topic, allowed to change through time. The topic structure is discovered
using BERTopic \parencite{Grootendorst_BERTopic} and the stance is read with natural language
inference (NLI) models \parencite{Burnham2023,Abercrombie2022}, both described in \hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}.
Section~\ref{sec:ch2} sets out the validation checks for these results: against the
Secretariat's hand-assigned topic categories, against the documented CRAMRA reversal, and
against observed co-authorship.
