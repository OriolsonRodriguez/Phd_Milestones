\chapter{Program and design of the research project}
\label{ch:program}

\section{Objectives, methodology and research plan}
\label{sec:objectives}

This thesis has three interconnected goals. The first is to reconstruct each party's preferences
from the regime's written records, as a directional quantification across topics and time (Chapter 1).
The second is to test whether the recovered preferences are credible: that is, whether they match known
historical positions and align with the ATCM decision record (Chapter 2). The third is to use the
validated preferences to simulate the regime's collective decision-making, so that its collective outputs,
including consensus failures, can be anticipated (Chapter 3).
Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} shows the overall flow of the research project.
For this project we define:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Topic} is a matter of collective attention among CPs. It is a noun: `mineral', `tourism'.
  \item \textbf{Axis} is the action on a matter. For example, `extraction' or `regulation'.
  \item \textbf{Issue} is a topic joined to an axis: a matter of attention that has become a question of contention. For example, `mineral extraction' or `tourism regulation'.
  \item \textbf{Stance} is a scalar that reflects the directional attitude (i.e. preference) towards an issue: a number on a line running from `for' to `against'.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Objective 1 / Chapter 1: The Support layer}
\label{sec:ch1}

\paragraph{Aim}
This thesis defines \textbf{support} as the CP stance aggregated over a year on a specific issue. This is computed by analysing the proposed WPs,
which are the inputs to the ATCM discussions. Support is positive when the WP argues that the community should act on the axis, and it is
negative when the WP argues against such action. The aim of this objective is to produce a defensible Support measure for all CPs over relevant issues through time.
For the ATCM case, this project uses WPs as the input data because, of all submitted documents, these are the only ones submitted by Parties or
 Observers (non-Secretariat) that require discussion and action. This makes WPs the most likely text to contain Parties' strategic intentions.

\paragraph{Approach}
The approach to objective one is a pipeline with multiple stages (full details and notation in \hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). The
first stages of the pipeline discover what the corpus is about: each document is embedded into the vector space
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:computational} (detailed in \hyperref[app:g]{Appendix~G}), and BERTopic
\parencite{Grootendorst_BERTopic} clusters the embedded corpus into a set of topics, each with a
centroid: the average embedding of its documents. The next stages derive the issues from the topics, then select the passages
relevant to each issue, gated by cosine relevance to the issue centroid. The issue-relevant
sentences are extracted and scored by the NLI classifier against hypotheses (e.g. country C supports stricter tourism rules),
producing a five-class distribution that ranges from strong opposition to strong support, i.e. the stance (see \hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E} for more details).

Finally, the previous result is aggregated into the support tensor $\mathbf{S}$, a three-way array indexed by country,
 issue, and year: the weighted sum of the stances of the WPs the country co-authored
that year (Equation~\ref{eq:aggregate}, \hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). This aggregation is a design choice of this
thesis rather than a result from prior work: the attribution weights follow two conventions,
full credit and fractional credit, each answering a different question (\hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}).

\paragraph{Validation strategy}
The approach is validated with three independent checks, each targeting a
different stage of the pipeline. A topic validation check compares the discovered topics against the categories the Secretariat
assigns to every WP. Agreement is measured by majority-category overlap: the share of papers in
each discovered topic that belong to that topic's most common official category. A historical
validation uses the minerals negotiations of the late 1970s and 1980s as documented ground
truth \parencite{jackson2021antarctica} and compares them to the recovered countries' stances extracted from the text.
 Finally, a co-authorship check tests the assumption that countries co-author a WP because they
share its topic and stance: within the same year and topic, countries whose recovered stances are
similar should be the ones that actually co-author papers, with stance similarity computed
excluding the paper being predicted.

So far, preliminary work on this chapter has been completed; the results are shown in \hyperref[app:h]{Appendix~H}.

\subsection{Objective 2 / Chapter 2: The Opposition layer and external validation}
\label{sec:ch2}

\paragraph{Aim}
Consensus fails through objection, so anticipating failure requires knowing who opposes what. Accordingly, the aim of this
objective is to measure the opposition signal (veto signal): a directional measure of a party's resistance to a proposal. It is not the inverse of the
support layer: the support layer captures programmatic disagreement (a party proposes one
direction while another proposes the contrary), whereas the \textit{veto signal} is the act of
objecting to a proposal on the table. Blocking is rarer and more consequential, and a party may
hold the opposite stance on a topic yet never block.

\paragraph{Approach}
As with the support layer, this chapter builds a pipeline. For the ATs case, the ATCMs' final
reports are the text input. Because consensus operates by the absence of objection, explicit vetoes are rarely recorded
(contentious proposals are usually withdrawn or softened before a formal block), so the relevant text is scarcer.
An LLM extracts the statements in which a country objects to a proposal. Each extracted statement yields a (\emph{country}, \emph{target}) pair, where the target is the set of issues being
blocked, with rationale and source quote. Taking the issues
from Chapter~1 and mapping targets back to them yields an opposition tensor $\mathbf{O}$ with the same $\text{Country} \times \text{Topic} \times \text{Year}$
structure, whose entries range from $0$ (not likely at all to veto) to $1$ (a certain veto). The
pair $(\mathbf{S}, \mathbf{O})$ constitutes the input to the third chapter.

\paragraph{Validation strategy}
This thesis uses the results from Chapters 1 and 2 to compute how likely an output is to be produced, as follows: issues with high absolute support and low veto signal
 are most likely to produce a Measure; those with low absolute support and low veto signal are likely to produce a Decision; and those with high veto signal, regardless
 of support, most likely produce no output or a Resolution. This thesis uses these likelihoods to match the outputs counted by \textcite{Gardiner2025} and 
\textcite{Carter_LimitsAntarctic} (see \hyperref[app:f]{Appendix~F}), validating the results so far.

The main risks in this chapter are the sparsity of text that can be directly attributed to an input, and the identification of the country that vetoes any 
proposal. A further limitation is the influence of external geopolitics, as in the case of the consensus breakdown in 2022 due to the war in Ukraine 
\parencite{Gardiner2025}. The cause of this type of exogenous influence is unlikely to be reflected in the Support and Opposition signals.

\hyperref[app:d]{Appendix~D} sketches the opposition layer pipeline and its external-validation step for the ATCM case study.


\subsection{Objective 3 / Chapter 3: The Simulation layer}
\label{sec:ch3}

\paragraph{Aim}
This thesis refers to the \textbf{status quo} as the current institutional settlement of an issue; every
status quo change made by the CPs re-polarises the axis (e.g. a change from `tourism regulation' to `tourism ban').
The aim of this objective is to simulate consensus processes with country-agents whose state is
parameterised by the empirically derived support and opposition from previous chapters. Success is measured by its ability
to reproduce historical trajectories, examine institutional counterfactuals, and predict future status quo changes.

\paragraph{Approach}
A MARL simulation is implemented to create a custom virtual negotiation environment where proposals are presented one at a time.
Each proposal is an input text that is about one issue. Then the agents (one per consultative party)
decide whether to object. Each agent's decision-making is based on its corresponding value contained in the $\mathbf{S}$ tensor,
its opposition history ($\mathbf{O}$), and metadata such as consultative status,
official language, and GDP. An agent's action on a presented proposal is binary: to object or not. Under
the consensus rule the proposal is adopted only if no Party objects, so a single objection vetoes
adoption. Each agent's reward combines three terms: it rewards adoption of outcomes the Party
supports, penalises adoption of outcomes it opposes, and subtracts a diplomatic cost for casting
an objection (equations in \hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). Every agent holds a veto, and consensus emerges from the trade-off
between blocking a proposal and bearing the cost of objecting.

\paragraph{Validation strategy}
By the time this part of the project is implemented, at least one ATCM will already have occurred (the ATCM takes place every year). That ATCM's data
will not be contained in the support or opposition tensors. Running the simulation forward therefore predicts unseen data, and this
prediction can be validated directly against the AT Secretariat's database. This thesis aims to predict those ATCMs' outputs.



\section{Resources and funding required}
\label{sec:resources}

The project uses openly published data from the ATs document database, open-source and/or
QUT-licensed software, and the QUT high-performance computing cluster. No additional funding is
required.
