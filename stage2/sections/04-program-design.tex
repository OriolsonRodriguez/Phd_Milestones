\chapter{Program and design of the research project}
\label{ch:program}

\section{Objectives, methodology and research plan}
\label{sec:objectives}

The countries that participate in the Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings (ATCMs), and that
may submit Working Papers (WPs) for discussion at these meetings, are known as Consultative
Parties (CPs). A WP is, in principle, a proposal in textual form. Only the CPs can block a
proposal from reaching consensus, by objecting to it, and a single objection is enough to prevent
adoption. For further details on how the ATCMs' consensus-based system operates, see \hyperref[app:f]{Appendix~F}.

Consensus regimes can stall, as the ATCM does. For example, the designation of the emperor
penguin as a protected species has been debated since 2022, and WPs have been submitted since
then, but no consensus has been reached
\parencite{ATCM46WP34,ATCM46FinalReport,ATCM47FinalReport}. Over a comparable period, the
emperor penguin population in Antarctica declined by an estimated 9.6\% between 2009 and 2018
\parencite{LaRue2024}. A stalled regime is therefore a problem on substantive matters.

A party seeking to get a proposal adopted must anticipate every CP's response to the WP. This
makes it important to propose text that is likely to be acceptable to all CPs. In these consensus
regimes there are no voting records from which to derive the countries' positions
\parencite{ATS2023RoP}. Moreover, a Working Paper serves to open a discussion, but who leads that
discussion, and what is said within the ATCM's working groups, is not recorded. What is available
is the paper trail: the Working Paper, the final report, and the Measures, Resolutions and
Decisions that follow \parencite{ATCM1995dec1}, all publicly available through the ATS website.

However, this database contains thousands of documents \parencite{Gardiner2024}, so reading them
manually is a real challenge. Manual reading also does not support a systematic analysis of the
past decades of ATCM deliberation. It is therefore difficult for external observers and for CPs
to establish who sets the agenda at the ATCM, how discussions are typically settled, and how
parties bargain with one another. Admittedly, countries hold their own internal records, but
these can be missing or biased, and recently accepted CPs do not hold the full record that
longstanding CPs do. A new approach that processes the text and is reproducible is therefore
needed.

Natural language processing (NLP) has emerged as a possible solution to this problem. The
approach has gained momentum in several international relations applications, where text is
analysed to extract parties' positions \parencite{Grimmer2013,Slapin2008,Baturo2017,Rheault2019}.
It allows a quantitative measure that is reproducible and feasible for other corpora in
political science and international relations.

This research project develops a decision-support tool for parties proposing changes to the
current settlement of particular matters in consensus-based international forums. The tool is constructed
from, and evaluated against, the documentary record of the ATCMs, which serves as the main case
study.

For this project I define:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Topic:} a matter of collective attention among Consultative Parties. It is a
  noun, e.g. `mineral'.
  \item \textbf{Axis:} the action on a \textit{topic}. For example, `extraction' or `regulation'.
  \item \textbf{Issue:} a \textit{topic} joined to an \textit{axis}: a matter of attention that
  has become a question of contention. For example, `mineral extraction'.
  \item \textbf{Stance:} a scalar value representing the directional attitude towards an
  \textit{issue}: a number on a line running from `for' (+1) to `against'(-1). For example, the USSR's
  \textit{stance} on mineral extraction is $+0.5$, that is, for.
  \item \textbf{Status quo:} the current institutional settlement of an \textit{issue}. For
  example, at the time a Party proposed to regulate mineral extraction, any company or country
  wishing to extract minerals in Antarctica had no framework or law to operate under. The
  \textit{status quo} for that \textit{issue}, at that time, was therefore that extraction was
  unregulated.
  \item \textbf{Proposal:} a text that puts forward a change to the \textit{status quo}. For
  example, a Working Paper proposing the regulation of mineral extraction.
  \item \textbf{Blocking statement:} a passage in a final report recording an objection by a CP,
  or a group of CPs, named or unnamed, that prevented a \textit{proposal} from being adopted at
  that meeting. For example: ``Many Consultative Parties stated that they did not consider
  Belarus to have fulfilled the requirements outlined in Article IX (2) of the Antarctic Treaty
  and in Decision 2 (2017)'' \parencite[para.~186]{ATCM47FinalReport}.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Chapter 1: Estimating Parties' Stance}
\label{sec:ch1}

\paragraph{Motivation}
Under the ATCM Rules of Procedure \parencite{ATS2023RoP}, the principal formal mechanism for
changing the \textit{status quo} is the submission of a Working Paper. These \textit{proposals}
are likely to contain their authors' views on how an \textit{issue} should be settled: a WP names
the \textit{issue} and expresses its authors' \textit{stance} on it. Recovering those
\textit{stances} offers one way to reveal patterns in the parties' positions, and in what they
treat as relevant and to what extent.

The recovered \textit{issues} and \textit{stances} would allow a proposing party to identify
possible allies in its efforts to change the \textit{status quo}. They would also provide the
quantitative basis on which more sophisticated tools can be built
(see \hyperref[sec:ch2]{Chapter~2} and \hyperref[sec:ch3]{Chapter~3}).

\paragraph{Substantive aim}
To describe each ATCM party's \textit{stances} on particular \textit{issues} relative to the
other parties, and to compare how these change over time.

\paragraph{Methodological objective}
To extract the authors' \textit{stances} from the Working Papers submitted by the parties, using
Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques.

\paragraph{Assumption}
The CPs express their \textit{stances} on particular \textit{issues} in the papers they submit.

\paragraph{Research questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
  \item Can an NLP pipeline recover the parties' \textit{stances} on particular \textit{issues}?
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item Which \textit{issues} can be extracted from the CPs' Working Papers across six decades
    of ATCM deliberation?
    \item Can a \textit{stance} per CP on each \textit{issue} be recovered from the WPs?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Can parties be grouped by the similarity of their \textit{stances} on a given
  \textit{issue}?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Approach}
The approach to answering RQ~(a) is to build a pipeline with multiple stages (full details and
notation in \hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). The first stages of the pipeline discover what the
corpus is about: each document is embedded into the vector space introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:computational} (detailed in \hyperref[app:g]{Appendix~G}). Then BERTopic
\parencite{Grootendorst_BERTopic} clusters the embedded corpus into a set of \textit{topics}.
Each \textit{topic} has a centroid: the average embedding of its documents.

The next stages derive the \textit{issues} from the \textit{topics}, then select the passages
relevant to each \textit{issue}, gated by cosine relevance to the \textit{issue} centroid. The
\textit{issue}-relevant sentences of each paper are scored in one pass by a Natural Language
Inference (NLI) classifier against two author-anchored hypotheses per \textit{issue} (that the
author supports it, and that the author opposes it). It outputs a distribution over three ordinal
classes: ``For'', ``Neutral'' or ``Against'', whose expected value is the \textit{stance} (see
\hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E} for more details).

RQ~(b) is answered by grouping similar \textit{stances} per \textit{issue}. Grouping by
\textit{stance} value provides a set of CPs that held a similar \textit{stance} at a given ATCM.
The grouping is checked against observed co-authorship: parties with similar \textit{stances} on
an \textit{issue} should be the ones that co-author papers on it.

Preliminary work on this chapter is complete. \hyperref[app:h]{Appendix~H} shows the results.

\subsection{Chapter 2: Estimating Parties' Blocking Behaviour and Validating}
\label{sec:ch2}

\paragraph{Motivation}
An ``episode'', in the computer science sense, is a single sequence of events in which agents
interact with their environment, taking the system from an initial state to a final one
\parencite[Section~3.3]{Sutton2018}. Under
this definition, the ATCM's deliberation over its history is a sequence of episodes. Some of
these have attracted the attention of external observers and scholars, who have documented them
in detail, such as the CRAMRA negotiations \parencite{jackson2021antarctica} and the designation
of the emperor penguin as a protected species
\parencite{Kubny2022,Cahill2026}. Because these episodes are
documented in the literature, independently of the ATCM record itself, they provide a point of
validation for analyses derived from that record.

Some ATCM episodes show the consensus-based system at its most characteristic: a
\textit{proposal} is put forward, discussed, rejected, and perhaps resubmitted at a later ATCM,
with the cycle repeating until the \textit{issue} is settled.

Before submitting a \textit{proposal}, a CP has no clear view of the objections other parties are
likely to raise, or of which parties will raise them. Experts and national delegations may hold
institutional memory and internal records that offer some indication, but these can be mistaken,
partial or lost. One way to form such a view is to estimate from the text how likely each party is to object.

\paragraph{Substantive aim}
To establish how likely a proposed change to the \textit{status quo} is to be blocked by specific
parties.

\paragraph{Methodological objective}
First, to identify \textit{blocking statements} in the ATCM final reports, together with the
parties that made them. Second, to estimate the probability that a party $c$ will block a
\textit{proposal} $d$ on \textit{issue} $k$. Third, to validate the \textit{stance} and blocking
probability measures against documented episodes.

\paragraph{Assumptions}
The final reports record \textit{blocking statements} in sufficient number and attributability
to build a predictive model with non-trivial accuracy. 


\paragraph{Research questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
  \item How can \textit{blocking statements}, and the parties that made them, be identified in
  ATCM final reports?
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item How reliably can \textit{blocking statements} be traced back to the \textit{proposals}
    that prompted them?
    \item What proportion of \textit{blocking statements} in final reports can be attributed to
    an identifiable party?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Can a blocking probability per party and \textit{issue} be estimated from the
  \textit{blocking statements}?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Approach}
The text used for this part of the project is the final reports (FRs) produced by the ATCM plenary
at every meeting, which record the decisions reached and the objections raised
\parencite{ATCM46FinalReport,ATCM47FinalReport}. Some passages state that a Party, or a group of
Parties, objects to a \textit{proposal}, and these \textit{blocking statements} also carry
information about what is being objected to.

RQ~(a) is answered in three steps. First, an LLM locates all \textit{blocking statements} in each
FR, an annotation task on which LLMs perform comparably to human coders
\parencite{Gilardi2023}. Second, the author of the objection is identified where the \textit{blocking statement} names
it explicitly, and marked as ``unknown'' otherwise. Third, the text is converted to a vector by
the same embedding model used in Chapter~1, and that vector is mapped to its nearest centroid from
Chapter~1.

The third step maps the text to the \textit{issues} it concerns. Where the \textit{issue} is not
explicitly mentioned, an auxiliary step maps the \textit{blocking statement} to the WP that
prompted it, and that WP gives the \textit{issue} (the WP-to-\textit{issue} mapping comes from
Chapter~1). This yields a set of (\textit{blocking statement}, vector, blocker names,
\textit{issue}) tuples, which answers RQ~(a).

RQ~(b) is answered from the same tuples. Where the blockers are unknown, the objection is
attributed to the plenary. From these tuples, a blocking probability per party is estimated using
density estimation techniques \parencite{Parzen1962}: the probability that party $c$ objects to a
\textit{proposal} on
\textit{issue} $k$, estimated from its objection frequency conditional on the \textit{issue} at a
given point in time.

Finally, the \textit{stances} from Chapter~1 and the blocking probability from Chapter~2 are
validated together by comparison with known episodes. The criteria are the grouping of countries
for and against the \textit{issue}, and the outcome at the end of the episode.


\subsection{Chapter 3: Simulating Consensus: Coalition Formation and Decision Support}
\label{sec:ch3}

\paragraph{Motivation}
A CP at the ATCM constantly faces the challenge of proposing changes that are minimally
acceptable to every other party. Meeting that challenge requires knowing what other parties have
proposed and rejected in the past. A simulation that indicates to a proposing party the likely
supporting group of parties and the likely opposing one would improve the chances of a
\textit{proposal} being adopted. A means of testing alternative wordings of the same
\textit{proposal} before submission would do the same.

\paragraph{Substantive aim}
To identify non-trivial patterns of interaction between the parties, and to predict the success
of a \textit{proposal} from its text.

\paragraph{Methodological objective}
To use \textit{stance} and blocking probability to build an agent-based simulation, and to
construct a decision-support tool aggregating both measures.

\paragraph{Assumptions}
The written record of the ATCMs is sufficient to model the consensus dynamics of interest.
Wider international politics enters the model only where it appears in the WPs and FRs.

\paragraph{Research questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
  \item Can \textit{stance} and blocking probability serve as inputs to an agent-based simulation
  that produces, for each \textit{issue}, a ranking of potential coalition partners for a
  proposing party?
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item Which type of agent-based simulation is most suitable given the available input
    parameters?
    \item Which outputs are useful to a proposing party and derivable from the available
    measures?
    \item How accurately do the resulting rankings predict the groups of parties for and against
    out-of-sample \textit{proposals} from recent ATCMs?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Approach}
A simulation provides a custom virtual negotiation environment in which \textit{proposals} are
presented one at a time. Each \textit{proposal} is an input text concerning a single
\textit{issue}. The agents, one per Consultative Party, then decide whether to object. Each agent
decides from its \textit{stance} on that \textit{issue}, its blocking probability, and metadata
such as consultative status, time as a CP, official language, and GDP.

Each agent's reward combines three terms: it rewards adoption of outcomes on which the party's
\textit{stance} is positive (for), penalises adoption of outcomes on which it is negative
(against), and subtracts a diplomatic cost for casting an objection (equations in
\hyperref[app:e]{Appendix~E}). Every agent holds a veto, and consensus emerges from the trade-off
between blocking a \textit{proposal} and bearing the cost of objecting.

By the time this part of the project is implemented, at least one ATCM will already have occurred
(the ATCM takes place every year). That ATCM's data will not be contained in the \textit{stances}
or the blocking probabilities. Running the simulation forward therefore predicts unseen data. This
thesis aims to predict those episodes: which groups of parties are for and against a given
\textit{proposal}. This answers RQ~(a).

\section{Resources and funding required}
\label{sec:resources}

The project uses openly published data from the ATs document database, open-source and/or
QUT-licensed software, and the QUT high-performance computing cluster. No additional funding is
required.

\section{Individual contribution to the research team}
\label{sec:contribution}

I am the sole researcher on this project, with guidance from my supervisory team.
The Chapter~1 infrastructure developed at the outset of candidature is presented not as thesis
results but as preliminary work establishing feasibility, and all reported findings will be
produced within candidature. The thesis extends prior group
work by \textcite{Botelho_CoalitionDynamics}, \textcite{Kumar_ConsistentPatterns} and \textcite{Carter_LimitsAntarctic}.

\section{Acknowledging use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and AI-assisted technologies}
\label{sec:ai}

I have used Claude Code for boilerplate and scaffolding code in the implementation of the
pipelines and scripts. All architectural decisions, methodological choices, and algorithm design
were specified by me in the input prompts. I have also reviewed and edited the generated code, and
I have reviewed its results manually.
Alongside conventional database searches (for example
Scopus) using search strings, with no AI feature enabled, I have used Consensus AI and the Claude
app to find relevant scientific papers in the available literature. These AI searches are based on
my own ideas, keywords, and criteria. Afterwards, I read the papers and drew the conclusions
myself. Finally, I have used Claude Code as a writing assistant for this document, for rewording
and for help with grammar, readability, clarity and LaTeX formatting. All research questions,
project design, ideas, interpretation of results, and selection of literature are my own.
