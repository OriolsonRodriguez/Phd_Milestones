\chapter{Background and Literature Review}
\label{ch:litreview}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:lr-intro}
Consensus is a collective decision-making process where every party can object to any
proposal and effectively veto it. A body becomes a consensus regime when consensus is
the only means to reach decisions. Consensus is not the same as unanimity: a unanimity
rule puts a proposal to a vote and records who votes in favour, against or abstain, whereas under
consensus a decision proceeds whenever no party registers a formal objection \parencite{Rietig2023}. 
Since a single party can halt any proposal, an outside observer might expect such regimes to
decide very little. The larger and more heterogeneous the group, the smaller the set of proposals that change the
status quo \parencite{Tsebelis2002}. In practice, consensus regimes can reach agreement even on contested,
transnational issues, often by relying on informal practices: trust, dialogue, and leadership. These practices fill the gaps left by
their formal rules \parencite{Sykorabodie2019}. But success is never guaranteed: in bodies such as the United Nations Security Council
a single veto routinely blocks collective action, and even historically productive regimes can stall \parencite{Gardiner2025}. 
Quantitatively, the picture is no different: what separates a consensus regime that keeps reaching decisions from one that can
no longer reach them is not captured by existing models of consensus dynamics \parencite{Perez2018,Tran2024}. A model that better reproduces
how consensus is reached or broken is therefore needed, both to explain past behaviour and to anticipate when a
regime approaches the limits of its capacity to act.

Models of multi-actor decision-making already exist, but they were not built for this task: they
represent each actor with a single, low-dimensional position and rarely use the actual text those
actors produce. Capturing a consensus regime faithfully requires positions across many issues,
read directly from the text the actors produce, and validated against observed outputs rather
than assumed. Recent advances in natural language processing and machine learning make each of
these upgrades feasible.

The Antarctic Treaty System (ATS) is one of those consensus-driven regimes. At its centre is the
Antarctic Treaty (AT), whose consultative parties meet yearly at the Antarctic Treaty
Consultative Meeting (ATCM), where outputs are adopted only by consensus \parencite{ATS2023RoP}.
In 2022, for the first time, the ATCM parties failed to reach consensus on the final report; even
as activity has increased, the regime has become less responsive to challenges \parencite{Gardiner2025}.


\section{Modelling consensus: responsive and anticipatory approaches}
\label{sec:consensus}
There are two traditional groups of models for how groups reach consensus. The first group is responsive and interactive: each actor repeatedly reacts to
the current positions of the others. These are the opinion-dynamics models. They treat consensus as \textit{convergence}: opinions are iteratively averaged until
they meet \parencite{DeGroot1974}, with several variants (Appendix~E).
Opinion-dynamics models can nevertheless test for agreement: declare consensus when all final
positions lie within a tolerance radius, as \textcite{Bernardo2021} do for the negotiation of the
2015 Paris Agreement. That test, however, captures preference convergence, not the consensus rule
the ATS uses: a procedural right under which one party can block a decision no matter how close
the remaining positions are.

The second group is anticipatory: each actor reasons about how the others will respond and
chooses its actions to maximise an objective function. Responsive agents, by contrast, follow an
update rule with no objective at all. These models use game theory to treat consensus as a
unanimity rule: in the veto-players framework of \textcite{Tsebelis2002}, the status quo is
replaced only when no party is left worse off (formal condition in Appendix~E). As parties
multiply and their positions spread, the set of adoptable policies therefore shrinks. Analysing
the ATS, \textcite{Gardiner2025} point to the same ingredients: a growing and diversifying
membership, divergent views, and a consensus rule, and read the regime's shift away from binding
agreements as declining performance.

Each group has strengths and limits. Responsive models are simple, can be scaled to many agents,
and are easy to calibrate against data. But their agents have no objective, no veto, and no
strategy. Anticipatory models
represent vetoes and strategic choice directly, and they explain \emph{why} a proposal passes or
fails. But their equilibria become hard to compute as parties and issues multiply, and they are
rarely confronted with data from real negotiations.

The ATS is this thesis's case study of such a regime: its Consultative Parties (CPs) decide
exclusively by consensus at the ATCM, and its best-documented episode of contested consensus is
CRAMRA, a minerals convention agreed in 1988 and then abandoned in favour of the Madrid
Protocol's mining ban \parencite{jackson2021antarctica}. That documented reversal provides a
canary test for any preference measure this thesis builds. The regime's mechanics, outputs, and the
full CRAMRA episode are described in Appendix~F \parencite{Gardiner2025}.

Between these two groups lies a middle ground: models that retain the strategic, veto-bearing
structure of the second group but learn equilibrium from repeated interaction instead of solving
for it. This field is treated in the next section.


\section{Negotiation and coalition formation simulation using reinforcement learning}
\label{sec:marl}

Section~\ref{sec:consensus} left two requirements for a model of ATS behaviour. The model must
represent many heterogeneous parties, each holding a veto and bargaining over a status quo. It
must also remain computable at that scale, which the closed-form equilibria of analytic game
theory are not. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) meets both requirements: it keeps the
game-theoretic substance but learns equilibrium behaviour from repeated interaction instead of
solving for it.

Formally, MARL is defined over stochastic (Markov) games, a generalisation of repeated games in
which the state of the negotiation changes as the agents act. Each agent observes the state,
chooses an action, and receives a reward: the objective function it learns to maximise. The
learning procedures approximate equilibrium behaviour over many simulated rounds rather than
deriving it in closed form \parencite{Littman1994}.

The field spans foundational bargaining and coalition-formation work
\parencite{Gronauer2022,Sarkar2022} and, most recently, agents based on Large Language Models
(LLMs) that model political negotiation directly (Appendix~E). However, these simulations run on
synthetic data or abstract benchmarks rather than the deliberative records of a real consensus
regime.


\section{Quantitative and text-based study of institutions and treaties}
\label{sec:computational}

The quantitative study of international institutions long predates its application to
Antarctica. One line of work codes the design of treaties and organisations as structured
variables, such as their decision rules, and links these variables to institutional performance,
survival, and decline \parencite{Gray2018}. A more recent trend treats the \emph{text} of
international agreements and debates as data \parencite{Grimmer2013}, recovering state positions
from voting records and speeches \parencite{Baturo2017,Slapin2008}. Both show that the
preferences and design features of treaty regimes can be measured, not merely described.

The ATS's health has been assessed qualitatively \parencite{Mancilla2023} and quantitatively, in
two ways. \textcite{Gardiner2025} measures the regime in aggregate, counting inputs, outputs, and
the time legally binding measures take to enter into force, and concludes that responsiveness to
challenges in the Antarctic region is declining. The second way is structural. A Working Paper
(WP), the document type through which parties bring proposals to the ATCM (Appendix~F), may be
submitted by a single party or co-sponsored by several, and this co-sponsorship has been used to
map who collaborates with whom through co-authorship networks of ATCM documents
\parencite{Botelho_CoalitionDynamics,Kumar_ConsistentPatterns}.
This thesis uses both lines of work as validation targets: parties with similar stances
(directional positions on a topic) are likely to co-sponsor papers together, and the same measures should help predict the output series
reported by \textcite{Gardiner2025} (Section~\ref{sec:ch2}).
Neither analysis, however, reads the content of the papers, so neither can say which issues
divide the parties, a limitation taken up in Section~\ref{sec:gap}.

A separate field does read the content: positional measures place each actor on a policy scale
using only the text the actor produces. It has two paradigms, which differ in how they turn text
into numbers: word counts (i.e. frequencies) or embeddings, vectors in which texts with similar
meaning end up close together. The word-count paradigm recovers a single, uninterpreted
dimension, which in a corpus as topically diverse as the ATCM Working Papers would likely
reflect what parties talk about rather than what they support. This thesis therefore adopts the
embedding paradigm: topics are discovered with BERTopic \parencite{Grootendorst_BERTopic}, and
stances are read with natural language inference (NLI) classifiers, which judge whether a text
supports or contradicts a stated hypothesis \parencite{Burnham2023,Abercrombie2022}. The result
is a topic-conditioned stance: a separate position per party and topic through time. The
supporting literature and definitions are reviewed in Appendix~G, and Section~\ref{sec:ch2} sets
out the validation checks.


\section{Summary of literature gap}
\label{sec:gap}

The descriptive work of Section~\ref{sec:computational} \parencite{Gardiner2025,Mancilla2023}
shows what the regime outputs but not who produces what. The structural analyses of co-authorship networks
\parencite{Botelho_CoalitionDynamics,Kumar_ConsistentPatterns} are undirected and unattributed.
The text-based positional methods of Section~\ref{sec:computational} recover latent positions
rather than topic-specific stances over time (Appendix~G). Finally, consensus has been simulated as convergence
(Section~\ref{sec:consensus}) or on synthetic data (Section~\ref{sec:marl}). These literatures differ
in method but none recovers a directional preference: who is for or against what, extracted from
the regime's own words.

This gap has two parts. The knowledge gap is the question raised in Section~\ref{sec:lr-intro}:
whether the regime's outputs, including its failures, can be anticipated from the parties'
positions at all. The methodological gap is
the missing instrument: no existing method measures each party's directional stance, per topic
and over time, from the regime's own records. Without that instrument the question cannot be
answered. Section~\ref{sec:rqs} turns this gap into three research goals.

The closest prior work shares parts of this design but not its object. Stance detection recovers
for-or-against positions from parliamentary debates \parencite{Abercrombie2022}, but not a
per-party, per-topic measure through time. \textcite{Moghimifar2024} drive a negotiation
simulation with preferences derived from party manifestos, but in a parliamentary coalition
setting, not a consensus regime where every party holds a veto.
No prior work extracts a directional preference for each party over time from a consensus
regime's own textual records, nor uses such preferences to drive a strategic simulation in which agents have veto
power. This thesis supplies both halves and joins them: the preferences recovered from the record become the
 parameters of the simulation that reproduces consensus and its failures.


\section{Research problem}
\label{sec:rqs}
The first goal of this thesis is to reconstruct parties' preferences from the written records
as a directional measure (stance) across topics and time. The second goal is to extract
a directional opposition (veto) signal from the same records, and to test whether the combined 
preferences recover known history and predict decisions. Finally, this thesis aims to use these preferences
to parameterise a multi-agent model that simulates consensus and its failures.
